Abstract
Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) improve task performance through extended
inference-time reasoning. Although previous studies suggest that longer reason-
ing should lead to more robust safety behavior, we find evidence to the contrary:
over-extended reasoning can instead be exploited to systematically weaken refusal
behavior. We propose Chain-of-Thought Hijacking, a simple yet effective black-
box jailbreak attack that induces LRMs to engage in prolonged benign puzzle-
solving reasoning, often lasting more than five minutes, before eliciting harmful
compliance. Across HarmBench, CoT Hijacking achieves attack success rates of
99%, 94%, 100%, and 94% on Gemini 2.5 Pro, ChatGPT o4 Mini, Grok 3 Mini,
and Claude 4 Sonnet, respectively. To understand why this attack succeeds, we
conduct activation probing, attention-pattern analysis, and causal interventions on
open-source reasoning models. Our results indicate that refusal behavior depends
on a low-dimensional safety signal whose expression weakens as reasoning traces
grow longer. In particular, extended benign reasoning shifts attention away from
harmful intentions and attenuates refusal-related activations, producing what we
call refusal dilution. These findings demonstrate that excessively prolonged rea-
soning can introduce a systematic jailbreak attack surface. We release our evalua-
tion materials to support reproducibility and further research.
1 Introduction
Large reasoning models (LRMs) extend standard language models by allocating inference-time
compute to generate step-by-step reasoning before producing an answer. This approach improves
performance across a range of tasks, including mathematics, programming, and scientific problem
solving [1–3]. Recent work has further suggested that longer reasoning can strengthen safety mech-
anisms by improving the consistency and robustness of refusal behavior [4–7]. This has led to an
emerging expectation: as LRMs reason longer, they should become not only more capable but also
safer.
Counterintuitively, in this study we find evidence that contradicts this expectation: rather than
strengthening safety mechanisms, over-extended reasoning can systematically weaken them under
our jailbreak construction. We propose Chain-of-Thought Hijacking (CoT Hijacking ), a jail-
break attack that induces target models to engage in excessively prolonged benign puzzle reasoning
(often >5 minutes) to attenuate refusal and elicit harmful compliance (see Figures 1 and 3). We
evaluate this attack on HarmBench [8] across four frontier models, achieving attack success rates
of 99% on Gemini 2.5 Pro, 94% on ChatGPT o4 Mini, 100% on Grok 3 Mini, and 94% on Claude
4 Sonnet. These results substantially exceed prior LRM-specific jailbreak methods: Mousetrap [9]
achieves 44%, H-CoT [10] achieves 60%, and AutoRAN [11] achieves 69% on Gemini 2.5 Pro.

To understand why CoT Hijacking succeeds, we investigate a mechanism we term refusal dilution:
as reasoning traces grow longer, refusal-related safety signals become progressively diluted in our
jailbreak setting. We first identify a refusal-related activation direction that provides a measurable
proxy for safety behavior in reasoning models. We then use this signal to quantify the relationship
between CoT length and refusal behavior, analyze layer-wise and head-wise attention patterns, and
perform targeted causal interventions on attention heads implicated by this analysis.
Our analysis reveals that refusal behavior depends on a low-dimensional safety signal whose ex-
pression weakens as reasoning sequences grow longer. Extended benign puzzle reasoning shifts
attention away from harmful requests, thereby weakening corresponding safety checks and atten-
uating refusal-related activations in the relevant layers. Targeted attention-head ablations further
show that specific heads play a causal role in this effect. These findings suggest that CoT Hijacking
exploits a systematic vulnerability in reasoning models rather than an isolated prompt-engineering
artifact. Mitigating this failure mode will require safety mechanisms that remain robust across long
reasoning traces. Our contributions are as follows:
• We introduce CoT Hijacking, a black-box jailbreak attack that exploits prolonged benign puzzle
reasoning to reduce safety reliability in LRMs.
• We demonstrate that CoT Hijacking achieves attack success rates of 99%, 94%, 100%, and 94%

2 Related Work
Large Reasoning Model To overcome the intrinsic limitation of fast system-1 thinking [12, 13],
with OpenAI o-series model [5] and DeepSeek-R1 [14] as two representatives, a surge of large
reasoning models has been proposed, focusing on enhancing system-2 thinking ability, or slow,
step-by-step thinking with chain-of-thoughts [1, 15, 16]. Trained with supervised fine-tuning or re-
inforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR)[17], large reasoning models exhibit a substan-
tial growth in the chain-of-thought length[18–20], which is usually associated with the emergence
of sophisticated reasoning abilities like reflection and self-correction [21]. However, as a side-effect
of reasoning-oriented training, recent studies find that stronger reasoning models are more likely
to produce harmful content, especially in the chain-of-thought, suggesting potential safety risk of
LRMs [22].
Jailbreaking Large Language Models Jailbreaking attacks target or bypass the internal safety
mechanisms of language models and induce them to produce harmful or unsafe content. Existing ap-
proaches to jailbreaking can be divided into two categories based on their access to the victim model.
To be more specific, white-box methods directly alter the parameters [23], activations [24], log-
its [25], or modify the prompt with gradient information [26]. On the other hand, black-box methods
simply rewrite the prompt into a seemingly benign query by designing a convoluted scenario [27],
creating a role-play context [28], enciphering the query into ciphertext or program code [29, 30],
translating it into multiple languages [31], or simply optimizing sampling strategies [32]. Com-
pared with general language models, LRMs introduce new vulnerability to jailbreaking attacks even
after sophisticated safety alignment [4]. An important factor could be the decline of instruction-
following ability after a long chain-of-thought [33] due to attention dilution [34]. Moreover, the
chain-of-thought can reveal the refusal criteria of LRMs, which can be exploited by attackers to
bypass the safety check [10].
Mechanistic Interpretability of Safety Mechanistic interpretability builds a causal connection
between the internal representation and the behavior of language models. Recent interpretability
studies on language model safety reveal safety mechanisms through component analysis [35] or
circuit analysis [36]. Specifically, previous work finds that the activation for harmful and harmless
prompts can be clustered with a clear boundary [37, 38] while the activation for jailbreaking prompts
are close to or even beyond the boundary, therefore misleading the harmfulness detection of language
models. Furthermore, the difference between the mean of two clusters, or refusal direction [39]
can be used for manipulating the refusal mechanism when added or subtracted from activation at
inference time [24, 40]. Most relevant to our study, He et al. [41], Zhou et al. [42] finds that safety-
related parameters are sparse and located within specific attention heads. Our findings also connect
to broader patterns where safety behaviors are subverted under pressure, such as sycophancy and
reward-tampering [43].
3 Chain-of-Thought Hijacking
Inspired by findings of previous work [44] that large reasoning models with overlong CoT exhibit
degraded performance in instruction-following, we first motivate this research with a pilot study on
the correlation between CoT length and jailbreak success rate (Section 3.1). Then we propose a
new jailbreak method CoT Hijacking that guides the language model to produce overlong CoT
(Section 3.2) and evaluate its efficacy empirically on HarmBench (Section 3.3).

is truncated to a short placeholder; Natural, where the model generates its default reasoning trace;
and Extended, where the prompt encourages long step-by-step reasoning beyond the model’s nat-
ural length. The experimental results for the pilot study is shown in Table 1. We can observe that
compliance increases from 27% in the Minimal condition to 80% in the Extended condition. This pi-

3.2 Jailbreak Methodology
Motivated by the length-dependent degradation observed above, we propose CoT Hijacking as
a prompt-based black-box attack tailored to reasoning models. Let x denote the original harmful
instruction and let π denote the victim reasoning model. Without attack, the model directly samples
a response as y ∼ π(· | x). CoT Hijacking instead uses an auxiliary model ϕ to construct a benign
puzzle preface p ∼ ϕ(· | x) conditioned on x, and then queries the victim model with the composed
input to obtain the response y ∼ π(· | p, x). In this way, the reasoning model is induced to generate
lengthy CoT for solving the benign puzzle before responding to the harmful request. Figure 2
summarizes the attacker-model prompt template used to construct such CoT Hijacking prompts.

To scale attack construction, we implement an automated pipeline using an attacker LLM (e.g.,
Gemini 2.5 Pro) to generate candidate prefaces and integrate payloads, as shown in Figure 3. The
pipeline follows a black-box feedback loop: at each iteration, the target model’s response is evalu-
ated for (i) whether it refuses and (ii) the resulting CoT length. These signals are then fed back to
the attacker model to refine the next candidate prompt, pushing the target toward longer reasoning
and harmful compliance. In our CoT Hijacking pipeline, prompts are deliberately framed as intri-
cate puzzles, with the malicious request subtly fused at the end. The target model interprets these
as demanding reasoning tasks that warrant substantial CoT budget, ultimately leading the model to
follow the harmful request embedded within the puzzle context. Because the attack relies on elic-
iting long reasoning traces, we focus on reasoning models throughout this work. Prompt examples
are shown in Figure 1.

measurable refusal-related signal in large reasoning models (LRMs). Following Arditi et al. [39],
we compute a refusal direction by contrasting mean residual activations on harmful and harmless
instructions. This direction serves as a low-dimensional proxy for refusal-related safety behavior.

4.1 Bidirectional Control via Activation Interventions
We test whether this refusal direction is causally involved in refusal behavior through two com-
plementary interventions: direction ablation on harmful instructions, which removes the refusal
direction from residual activations and should increase harmful compliance if refusals rely on this
direction; and direction addition on harmless instructions, which adds the direction to residual
activations and should induce over-refusal if the direction is sufficient to trigger refusal.
The interventions produce the expected bidirectional effects. On harmful instructions, ablating the
refusal direction sharply increases harmful compliance: ASR rises from 11% to 91%. On harmless
instructions, adding the refusal direction has the opposite effect: benign compliance drops from 94%
to 1%, indicating substantial over-refusal.
Together, these results show that refusal in Qwen3-14B is governed by a low-dimensional activation-
space signal during CoT generation. This extends the refusal-direction finding of Arditi et al. [39]
from standard language models to reasoning models with structured CoT generation, and establishes
the direction as a measurable and causally relevant safety signal for the mechanistic analysis that
follows.
4.2 CoT Hijacking Mechanism: Refusal Dilution
Prior work shows that refusal behavior can be manipulated directly through white-box activation
editing along the refusal direction [39]. CoT Hijacking differs in that it does not require access
to model internals. Instead of explicitly editing activations, our black-box attack induces overlong
benign puzzle reasoning that weakens the formation or expression of the refusal-related signal. We
refer to this effect as refusal dilution.
During inference, the next-token activation reflects attention over prior tokens. Tokens of harmful
intent amplify the refusal direction, while benign tokens diminish it. By forcing the model to gen-
erate an excessively long chain of benign puzzle reasoning, harmful tokens make up only a small
fraction of the attended context (as shown in Figure 22 and Appendix L). In this sense, the refusal
signal is diluted below threshold, allowing harmful completions to slip through.

5.1 Defining the Refusal Component
Building on prior work [39], we quantify refusal behavior by projecting residual activations onto
the refusal direction vector. For each prompt, we extract the residual activation of the final input
token and compute its component along this vector: R = ⟨hlast, vrefusal⟩, where hlast is the residual
activation and vrefusal is the normalized refusal direction. We refer to R as the refusal component:
a scalar signal representing the model’s safety check at that position.
5.2 Effect of Chain-of-Thought Length
To systematically isolate the effect of CoT length, we analyze the refusal component in Qwen3-14B
across six CoT lengths (1k–47k tokens) and three instruction types (harmless, harmful, and stealthy
harmful; see Appendix E), as shown in Figure 4. For all three instruction types, we insert an identical
puzzle-solving prompt before all requests, and CoT toward puzzle reasoning is manually truncated
to obtain different CoT lengths.
These results in Figure 4 reveal three consistent patterns. (1) The refusal component remains near
zero in early layers and only becomes pronounced after approximately layer 15, indicating that
refusal behavior is primarily encoded in later layers. (2) For both harmful and stealthy harmful
instructions, increasing CoT length consistently reduces the refusal component, with the strongest
reduction observed in late layers. (3) Harmless instructions are largely insensitive to CoT length,
validating the refusal component as a safety-specific signal. This observation aligns with our be-

supports the proposed mechanism of refusal dilution. (A similar refusal component pattern is also

6 Results and Discussion
Mechanistic evidence: Section 4 shows that refusal is mediated by a low-dimensional signal (the
refusal direction) even in reasoning-augmented architectures. However, this signal is fragile: long
chains of benign reasoning dilute refusal-related activation, while attention shifts away from harmful
tokens. Our findings directly challenge the assumption that “longer reasoning improves robustness”
[4]. Instead, scaling inference-time reasoning can exacerbate safety failures, especially in models
explicitly optimized for long CoT. This calls into question alignment strategies that rely on shallow
refusal heuristics without mechanisms that scale with reasoning depth.
Toward mitigation: The systematic nature of CoT Hijacking suggests that prompt-specific patching
is unlikely to be sufficient. Effective defenses may require deeper integration of safety into the
reasoning process itself, such as monitoring refusal activation across layers, penalizing dilution, or
enforcing attention to harmful spans regardless of reasoning length. We hope our findings motivate
alignment strategies that are robust not only to short adversarial prompts but also to long chains of
reasoning.
7 Conclusion
We introduced CoT Hijacking, a simple jailbreak attack against reasoning models. By padding
harmful requests with long benign reasoning and a final-answer cue, we achieve high attack success
rates across both open and proprietary LRMs.Through mechanistic analysis we found refusal com-
ponents encode both the strength of safety checking in middle layers and the outcome of verification
in later layers. Long CoT hijacking suppresses these signals, shifting attention away from harmful

hance safety–can under certain conditions reduce a model’s refusal reliability. We believe that un-
derstanding and correcting this failure mode is essential for safely deploying reasoning-augmented
systems. We emphasize that this method should not be used for malicious purposes. Disclosing
vulnerabilities in a controlled and evidence-based manner is a standard practice in security research.
Making these failure modes visible allows developers, policymakers, and the research community
to understand and mitigate them. We believe that transparency, combined with early notification
to affected developers, is the most responsible path toward improving the safety and reliability of AI
systems, and we encourage others to follow similar responsible disclosure practices.

A Use of Language Models
Language models are used in our study as general-purpose assistance tools, and we use them to
check grammar mistakes and fix LaTeX compile errors.
B Limitations
Our experiments focus on text-based LRMs and a subset of benchmark tasks (HarmBench). Evalu-
ation relies on automated judges, which—although stricter than those used in prior work—may still
under- or over-estimate refusal. Moreover, we study refusal primarily through one mechanistic lens
(activation directions and attention), leaving open the possibility of complementary pathways for
safety failures.
C Impact Statement
This paper identifies and analyzes a vulnerability in large reasoning models, which we refer to as
Chain-of-Thought Hijacking. The proposed attack exploits extended benign reasoning to increase
the likelihood of harmful compliance, and therefore has clear dual-use implications. Our objective
is to improve the security and robustness of deployed reasoning systems, rather than to facilitate
misuse.
To reduce the risk of irresponsible use, we adopt several safeguards.
Responsible Disclosure. Prior to public release, we notified several affected model providers and
shared relevant technical details to support evaluation and mitigation efforts. We will continue to
coordinate with providers when appropriate.
Research-Only Release. We do not release harmful payloads, agentic exploit code, or system-
targeted automation. Any accompanying code is limited to controlled evaluation settings and benign
demonstration prompts, with the goal of supporting reproducible safety research.
Scientific Motivation. A central motivation of this work is to show that extended chain-of-thought
reasoning, which is often assumed to improve safety and deliberation, can under certain conditions
reduce refusal reliability. Understanding this failure mode is important for the safe deployment of
reasoning-augmented models.
We emphasize that this method should not be used for malicious purposes. We view controlled,
evidence-based disclosure of model vulnerabilities as an important part of AI safety research. By
making such failure modes visible, this work aims to help model developers, policymakers, and the
broader research community better understand and mitigate risks associated with reasoning-capable
systems.
D Reproducibility Statement
To support reproducibility, we describe the experimental setup, evaluation protocol, model configu-
rations, and implementation details in Section 3. We release research code for controlled evaluation
and benign demonstration purposes. The released materials are intended solely to facilitate indepen-
dent verification and further safety research.
E Evaluation Details
Judges. Unless otherwise noted, attack success rate (ASR) is evaluated using Gemini 2.5 Pro as
the automated judge. We adopt the JailbreakBench unified evaluation standard [46] for consistency.
In interpretability parts, we also report substring matching and DeepSeek-v3.1 judges in Section 4.
Prompts. CoT Hijacking prompts follow the template in Section 3.2, consisting of optional role-

available in our released code repository.
