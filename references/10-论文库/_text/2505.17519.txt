Chain-of-Lure: A Universal Jailbreak Attack Framework using
Unconstrained Synthetic Narratives
Wenhan Chang, Tianqing Zhu, Member, IEEE, Yu Zhao, Shuangyong Song, Ping Xiong*, Wanlei Zhou, Life
Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—In the era of rapid generative AI development,
interactions with large language models (LLMs) pose increas-
ing risks of misuse. Prior research has primarily focused on
attacks using template-based prompts and optimization-oriented
methods, while overlooking the fact that LLMs possess strong
unconstrained deceptive capabilities to attack other LLMs. This
paper introduces a novel jailbreaking method inspired by the
Chain-of-Thought mechanism. The attacker employs mission
transfer to conceal harmful user intent within dialogue and
generates a progressive chain of lure questions without rely-
ing on predefined templates, enabling successful jailbreaks. To
further improve the attack’s strength, we incorporate a helper
LLM model that performs randomized narrative optimization
over multi-turn interactions, enhancing the attack performance
while preserving alignment with the original intent. We also
propose a toxicity-based framework using third-party LLMs
to evaluate harmful content and its alignment with malicious
intent. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method
consistently achieves high attack success rates and elevated
toxicity scores across diverse types of LLMs under black-box
API settings. These findings reveal the intrinsic potential of
LLMs to perform unrestricted attacks in the absence of robust
alignment constraints. Our approach offers data-driven insights
to inform the design of future alignment mechanisms. Finally,
we propose two concrete defense strategies to support the
development of safer generative models. Our code is available
at https://github.com/ChangWenhan/chain-of-lure-official

Attack, Safety Alignment.
I. INTRODUCTION
Rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) [1],
[2] has greatly improved work efficiency, but has also in-
troduced critical security risks [3]. One essential concern of
LLM is jailbreak attacks [4], wherein attackers may craft
adversarial prompts to bypass the model’s safeguards and lead
to harmful or unintentional results. Such attacks compromise
the reliability of LLMs and may facilitate misinformation,
privacy breaches, or other malicious uses [5]. Investigating
jailbreaking techniques is therefore essential to thoroughly
assess model robustness, uncover post-training vulnerabilities,
and identify systemic flaws in safety alignment [6]–[8].
Based on the attacker’s level of access to the model,
jailbreaking attacks on LLMs can be divided into white-box
settings and black-box settings [9]. In white-box settings,
attackers have full visibility into the model’s architecture and
parameters. This allows for sophisticated strategies such as

vent safety mechanisms [5], [10]. Although highly effective,
these methods are resource-intensive and rely on insider-level
access, making them less applicable in practice, especially for
closed-source models. In contrast, black-box settings do not
require internal knowledge of the model. Instead, they exploit
vulnerabilities through carefully designed input prompts, using
techniques such as iterative refinement, semantic manipulation,
or psychological framing to subvert alignment constraints [11],
[12]. Although generally less precise than white-box methods,
black-box attacks present a greater practical risk in real-world
scenarios, as they can be executed by any user with access to
the model’s public interface.
Despite growing research interest, the practical value of
many current jailbreaking methods remains limited. While
these methods do exploit underlying weaknesses in safety
alignment, their heavy reliance on computationally expensive,
exhaustive trial-and-error often hinders their generalizability
and efficiency. Even with full access in white-box settings [13],
[14], attackers face significant challenges: the vast token search
space demands high computational cost, and the methods re-

search ranges. Moreover, they cannot be applied to closed-

Black-box attacks [15], [16], though more practical, depend on

whether safety alignment has been truly compromised. Many
current jailbreaks are limited by their focus on targeting
granular vulnerabilities via optimization-based trial-and-error,
a strategy that treats alignment as a static obstacle while

Chain-of-Thought show that LLMs can be jailbroken step-
by-step through multi-question prompting, enhancing both
efficiency and harmfulness.

progressively compromising its safety alignment. While CoT

known as safety alignment to ensure their outputs adhere to hu-

LLM operates as a conditional probability model, trained to
predict the next token xt+1 given a context Xt = (x1, . . . , xt)
by modeling the probability distribution P (xt+1 | Xt). When

To address this, alignment techniques remold the model’s
behavior. A primary approach is training-time alignment,
most notably accomplished through Reinforcement Learning
from Human Feedback (RLHF). Instead of acting as a post-
processing step, RLHF retrains the LLM by fine-tuning its
internal policy π, optimizing it to maximize the expected
score from a reward model Reward(X, Y ), which reflects
human preferences. Formally, the objective is to maximize
EY ∼π(·|X)[Reward(X, Y )].
An alternative strategy is inference-time intervention, which
acts as a safeguard after content generation. This approach can
be modeled as a filtering function ψ : Y → Ysafe that maps
the model’s raw output to a verified safe subset, i.e., Yaligned =
ψ(LLM(X)). Such interventions are typically implemented as
rule-based filters or external safety classifiers. Through these
methods, safety alignment transforms the LLM from a pure
sequence predictor into a constrained generator that operates
within the bounds of human-defined principles.
B. Jailbreak Attacks
While safety alignment measures are essential, they are
not infallible. LLMs remain susceptible to jailbreak attacks,
which are adversarial techniques designed to circumvent these
safeguards and produce prohibited outputs. The core of a
jailbreak is to craft a malicious prompt, Xadv, that embeds
a harmful user intent which the model is trained to refuse.
These adversarial prompts are engineered to exploit vulner-
abilities in the model’s safety training. Let a safety guardrail
be represented by a binary function ϕ, where ϕ(Y ) = 0
indicates an unsafe response Y . A straightforwardly malicious
prompt Xdirect would typically be blocked by the model (i.e.,
ϕ(LLM(Xdirect)) = 1), whereas its adversarial counterpart
Xadv is a modified version of Xdirect specifically crafted to
bypass this safeguard and elicit a prohibited output from the
model. Attackers employ various strategies to create such
prompts, ranging from complex role-playing scenarios and
prefix injections to automated, optimization-based prompt dis-
covery. Jailbreak fundamentally exploits the inconsistencies
in an LLM’s alignment, leveraging the model’s generative
capabilities against its own safety protocols.

III. RELATED WORKS
A. White-box Jailbreak Attack
White-box jailbreak methods refer to scenarios in which the
attacker has complete knowledge of the model’s architecture,
parameters, and training data. This full access allows attackers
to modify internal components of the model directly, such
as adjusting weights or altering code, to bypass security
mechanisms or generate harmful outputs.
For example, Zuo et al. [5] introduce the Greedy Coor-
dinate Gradient (GCG) method, which optimizes adversarial
prompts by iteratively replacing tokens in the prompt suffix
to maximize the likelihood of eliciting harmful or undesirable
responses from the models. Liu et al. [10] introduce AutoDAN,
a gradient-based attack that merges manual and automated
jailbreaks, producing readable prompts with high success
rates while evading perplexity-based detection. Jia et al. [20]
propose an improved method called I-GCG, which incorpo-
rates several innovations, including diverse target templates
to mislead LLMs and an automatic multi-coordinate updating
strategy to accelerate convergence. Liao et al. [21] developing
a generative model that produces adversarial suffixes designed
to be universally applicable across different models, making
them highly transferable.
White-box methods excel at deriving jailbreak prompts via
gradients or training strategies but suffer from long exploration
times, parameter sensitivity, and poor readability, making
black-box methods more practical in real-world settings.
B. Black-box Jailbreak Attack
A central challenge in the safety alignment of current LLMs
is that the resulting alignment is often superficial and fragile.
The alignment process frequently results in a “shallow safety
alignment” [22], primarily teaching the model to generate
refusal prefixes within the first few output tokens, without
fundamentally altering the vast, underlying knowledge base
from pre-training, which exhibits “elasticity” and a tendency to
revert to its original behaviors [23]. This superficiality creates
a critical vulnerability: in a black-box scenario where attackers
have no access to internal model parameters, their objective
becomes crafting inputs that exploit the inconsistencies within
this fragile safety alignment.
For example, Perez et al [24] introduced an automated red-
teaming method that uses a ”red” language model to generate a
high volume of discrete, single-turn prompts, efficiently stress-
testing a target model to uncover direct content-level flaws.
However, While effective at assessing reactions to isolated
inputs, it is less equipped to counter more advanced, narrative-
based attacks. The classical method PAIR [4] performs iter-
ative prompt refinement through structured attacker–evaluator
feedback, and TAP [16] extends it into a tree-search framework
with branching and pruning to explore multiple attack paths.
However, both rely on explicit embedding of harmful content
and fragile JSON-style formatted outputs, making their attacks
detectable and easily disrupted by format errors. In contrast,
Chain-of-Lure hides malicious intent via mission transfer
into adaptive narrative lure chains, achieving semantic-level
deception and format-independent multi-turn optimization for
more robust and stealthy jailbreaks.
Moreover, Liang et al. [25] proposed a weak-to-strong auto-
mated attack, where a less-aligned model generates narrative
prompts and refines them by analyzing the victim LRM’s
reasoning. Its creativity is limited by reliance on templates,
and it depends on exposed reasoning steps, failing if these
are hidden. Recently, Ren et al. [26] identified a safety
vulnerability in LLMs: brittleness to natural distribution shifts,
where semantically related yet seemingly harmless prompts
bypass safety mechanisms. While their theoretical insights
align with our observations, their method ActorBreaker relies
on multi-turn, step-by-step prompting to explore semantic vul-
nerabilities, but frequent path adjustments during interaction
hinder efficient and complete exploitation.
Our method exemplifies a strong adaptive black-box attack.
Its core innovation is that it does not rely on any fixed
attack templates or simple prompt modifications, thus avoiding
the weakness of being easily learned and defended against.
Instead, our approach leverages a third-party attacker model to
dynamically generate a complete, logically coherent narrative
lure chain with progressively guided questions. This strategy
shifts the confrontation from direct “prompt breaking” to a
more sophisticated “narrative seduction,” exploiting the LLM’s
intrinsic vulnerability of inadequate comprehension of the
users’ implicit intent.
IV. CHAIN-OF-LURE JAILBREAKING METHOD
A. Threat Model
In our Chain-of-Lure attack simulation, three key entities are
involved: the Attacker, Victim, and Helper. Their capabilities
are as follows:
• Attacker Model: The attacker model is a LLM with text
generation capability. It constructs a narrative based on
specific chained rules to bypass the victim model’s secu-
rity constraints while maintaining the original target of
the sensitive question. This narrative includes a series of
refined sub-questions. The attacker can ultimately derive
a method to solve the sensitive question by prompting the
victim model to answer these sub-questions, achieving
jailbreaking.
• Victim Model: The victim model is the LLM targeted by
the attack. Since different models undergo varying levels
of security reinforcement during training, their sensitivity
to sensitive problems differs, leading to varying degrees
of self-defense capability.
• Helper Model: The helper model refines the narrative
when the attacker model fails to execute the attack. It
can be the attacker model itself or an external model with
greater capabilities. If the victim model rejects the first
single-turn the narrative lure, the malicious optimization
task becomes a pure text optimization problem.

tured narrative lure chain. This approach transforms sensi-
tive or restricted questions into compelling, narrative-driven

C. Single-turn Interaction: Mission Transfer and Narrative
Lure
The Mission Transfer and Narrative Lure method introduces
an indirect yet highly effective approach to eliciting responses
from a victim model, where “indirect” indicates that the
prompt excludes the original harmful question but still induces
a response directly aligned with it. Our method leverages
LLMs’ advanced narrative generation capabilities to construct
contextually rich and plausible scenarios autonomously, em-
bedding sensitive questions within a logically coherent narra-
tive chain. By strategically structuring these lure chains, the
method circumvents explicit content restrictions and enables
the gradual extraction of detailed information.
The effectiveness of this approach is rooted in two core prin-
ciples: Mission Transfer and Narrative Lure. Mission Transfer
refers to transforming a sensitive question into a seemingly
innocuous but contextually relevant scenario, reducing the
likelihood of rejection by the victim model.
Narrative Lure ensures that the generated scenario remains
compelling and immersive, forming a lure chain, a structured
progression of details that gradually steers the conversation to-
ward the intended topic without triggering content moderation
safeguards.
Given an original sensitive question qo, the attacker model
autonomously synthesizes a structured narrative scenario s, ef-
fectively disguising sensitive intent. This construction process
is described in Algorithm 1.
At the core of Mission Transfer is the transformation
function C(qo), which models the LLM’s ability to construct
a narrative from the initial sensitive question: s = C(qo),
where s represents the generated scenario that embeds the
original sensitive intent. This function ensures that the original

D. Multi-turn Interaction: Chain Optimization
While the single-turn Mission Transfer and Narrative Lure
method can successfully jailbreak the victim model within
a single interaction, it may fail when the victim model
enforces strong security barriers during its training phase. In
such cases, the attacker model must refine the narrative lure
chain iteratively, progressively modifying the guiding details,
role assignments, and question sequencing while maintaining
logical coherence.
This multi-round refinement forms the second layer of
our approach, implementing a chain-based optimization that
enhances the narrative’s effectiveness until the victim model
provides the intended response.

After completing the CoL attack, we define T S to assess the
harmfulness of the model’s output. T S is calculated based on
the OpenAI Policy and our specific scoring rules, ranging from
1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater harm. This metric
quantifies the potential harm of the text, enabling analysis
and comparison of toxicity across different scenarios. The
evaluation is conducted using a third-party LLM, scoring the
alignment and harmfulness of the model output compared to
the original sensitive question, which provides a more accurate
reflection of the victim model’s jailbreaking extent.

By fusing these two stages, Algorithm 3 forms a coherent
framework that constructs an initial lure chain and adaptively
optimizes it across multiple rounds, ultimately producing a
robust sequence capable of bypassing model defenses.

E. RQ4: Importance of Toxicity Score in Differentiated Eval-
uation
Traditional jailbreak evaluations often rely on refusal key-
word detection, which tends to misrepresent attack effec-
tiveness. This approach may overestimate success when the
model avoids flagged terms without truly breaking alignment,
or underestimate it when benign contexts trigger keyword
matches. In contrast, using a fixed model to score response
toxicity enables a more nuanced assessment. The T S metric
captures both the harmfulness of the response and its semantic
alignment with the original sensitive query—crucial not only
for distinguishing superficial bypasses from genuine jailbreaks,
but also for revealing meaningful differences in the actual
effectiveness of competing attack methods.
For instance, consider the results on the AdvBench dataset.
In Table II, the white-box method GCG achieves a relatively
high ASR of 0.93 on Vicuna-7B; however, in Table II, its
corresponding T S is only 3.04, indicating that while the
method frequently bypasses keyword-based filters, the gener-
ated responses do not effectively capture the intended sensitive
content. Similarly, on AdvBench, Darkcite attains an ASR of
0.95 on Vicuna-7B, yet its T S is 3.72, which is noticeably
lower than the T S achieved by our CoL methods.
A similar pattern occurs in GPTFuzz (Table II): although
Darkcite achieves an ASR of 0.97, its T S remains relatively
low at 3.98, indicating that these ostensibly successful jail-
breaks are less harmful and less aligned with the original
sensitive queries. In contrast, our CoL methods show a better
balance between bypass success and response quality: CoL
Single-turn achieves an ASR of 0.99 with a T S of 4.82, while
CoL Multi-turn reaches a perfect ASR of 1.00 and a high T S
of 4.83. These results emphasize that evaluating the actual
responses of the victim model reveals attack effectiveness
more accurately than keyword-based detection, capturing both
the ability to bypass safety mechanisms and the extent to
which responses reflect the intended malicious content.
By incorporating T S into the evaluation framework, we can
assess not just whether a safety mechanism is bypassed, but
whether the resulting responses are genuinely harmful. This
distinction highlights a limitation of many baseline methods:
although they often achieve high ASR, their low T S scores
suggest superficial or unconvincing attacks.
VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, our research on LLMs reveals a disruptive
phenomenon: models are not only potential victims, but can
also actively act as attackers, systematically exploiting other
models through self-generated narrative strategies. The pro-
posed Chain-of-Lure method transforms this threat into an
actionable attack framework—embedding sensitive issues into
a progressive narrative scenario via “mission transfer” and
using multi-turn chain optimization to break through defenses.
This dual-chain design (narrative lure chain within a single-
turn + optimization chain between multi-turn interactions)
demonstrates that LLMs with weak security can leverage their
text generation advantages to become effective attack tools.
This finding challenges the conventional one-way view of
the attack–defense relationship. Our observations suggest that
weaker defenses may correlate with a greater capacity for
generating inductive narratives that can be weaponized. The
success of Chain-of-Lure not only exposes the vulnerability
of existing security mechanisms to multi-round narrative in-
duction but also highlights the “double-edged sword” nature
of LLM capabilities: their strong contextual coherence and
logical construction ability, which serve human needs, can also
be autonomously converted into an attack tool. Future research
needs to establish a new evaluation system while developing
dynamic detection techniques to identify potential induction
patterns within narrative chains.

Chain-of-Lure: A Universal Jailbreak Attack Framework using
Unconstrained Synthetic Narratives
Wenhan Chang, Tianqing Zhu, Member, IEEE, Yu Zhao, Shuangyong Song, Ping Xiong*, Wanlei Zhou, Life
Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—In the era of rapid generative AI development,
interactions with large language models (LLMs) pose increas-
ing risks of misuse. Prior research has primarily focused on
attacks using template-based prompts and optimization-oriented
methods, while overlooking the fact that LLMs possess strong
unconstrained deceptive capabilities to attack other LLMs. This
paper introduces a novel jailbreaking method inspired by the
Chain-of-Thought mechanism. The attacker employs mission
transfer to conceal harmful user intent within dialogue and
generates a progressive chain of lure questions without relying on
predefined templates, enabling successful jailbreaks. To further
improve the attack’s strength, we incorporate a helper LLM
model that performs randomized narrative optimization over
multi-turn interactions, enhancing the attack performance while
preserving alignment with the original intent. We also propose
a toxicity-based framework using third-party LLMs to evaluate
harmful content and its alignment with malicious intent. Ex-
tensive experiments demonstrate that our method consistently
achieves high attack success rates and elevated toxicity scores
across diverse types of LLMs under black-box API settings. These
findings reveal the intrinsic potential of LLMs to perform unre-
stricted attacks in the absence of robust alignment constraints.
Our approach offers data-driven insights to inform the design of
future alignment mechanisms. Finally, we propose two concrete
defense strategies to support the development of safer generative
models.
Index Terms—Jailbreak, Large Language Models, Black-box
Attack, Safety Alignment.

an optimized suffix using a combination of greedy and
gradient-based search.
• MAC (Momentum Accelerated GCG) attack enhances
the GCG attack by integrating a momentum term into
the gradient heuristic.
• DAN, which stands for “Do Anything Now,” is an early
and widely spread jailbreak prompt that operates by
instructing the LLM to act as a different character who is
repeatedly emphasized as not needing to adhere to content
policies or ethics.
• TAP (Tree of Attacks with Pruning) is an automated,
black-box, and interpretable jailbreaking method that uses
an attacker LLM to iteratively branch out candidate
prompts and an evaluator LLM to prune ineffective ones.
• DRA (Disguise and Reconstruction Attack) is a low-
resource, black-box method that exploits safety fine-
tuning biases by disguising a harmful instruction and
using payload reconstruction and context manipulation to
make the LLM jailbreaking.
• Darkcite is an adaptive, black-box jailbreak method that
exploits an LLM’s inherent bias toward authority by
matching optimal citation types to specific risks and
generating authoritative citations relevant to harmful in-
structions.

#### a) **Covering Digital Footprints:**

trails on both the infected systems and the networks it communicates with. Rootkits or malware could be used to
manipulate these logs.
