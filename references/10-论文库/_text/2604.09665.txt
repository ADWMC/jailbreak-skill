While the wide adoption of refusal training in large language models (LLMs) has showcased improvements
in model safety, recent works have highlighted shortcomings due to the shallow nature of these alignment
methods. To this end, the work on Deliberative alignment proposed distilling reasoning capabilities from
stronger reasoning models, thereby instilling deeper safety in LLMs. In this work, we study the impact of
deliberative alignment in language models. First, we show that despite being larger in model size and stronger
in safety capability, there exists an alignment gap between teacher and student language models, which
affects both the safety and general utility of the student model. Furthermore, we show that models aligned
through deliberative alignment can retain unsafe behaviors from the base model despite learning the reasoning
patterns of larger reasoning models. Building upon this observation, we propose a BoN sampling method
that attributes the unsafe behavior back to the base LLMs in the latent space, thereby down-ranking unsafe
responses to gain a meaningful improvement in model safety across multiple safety benchmarks with minimal
loss in utility. In particular, across 7 teacher models and 6 student models of different classes and sizes, we
show an average attack success rate (ASR) reduction of 28.2% in DAN, 31.3% in WildJailbreak and 35.4% in
StrongREJECT benchmarks. We further show that these safety gains prevail post RL training, thus highlighting
the uncertainty in safety reasoning and it’s explicit attribution to the base model.

1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) (Guo et al., 2025) have demonstrated remarkable advancements in reasoning,
safety and, capability largely driven by post training alignment methods (Bai et al., 2022, Ouyang et al.,
2022, Rafailov et al., 2024, Guan et al., 2025). While these alignment methods have shown early success
in inducing effective safety refusals recent works (Qi et al., 2024) have show the shallow nature of these
alignment methods thus enabling effective attacks that aims to bypass of these refusal behaviors (Zou et al.,
2023).
To this end with the raising traction towards reasoning models that leverages stronger chain of thought
(CoT) (Guo et al., 2025) the work of Guan et al. (2025) have proposed on instilling reasoning capabilities
from reasoning LLMs into non reasoning LLMs thereby enabling deeper safety on language models (OpenAI
et al., 2024). These alignment methods as seen in Figure 1 starts with creating golden reasoning traces
from stronger reasoning LLMs aided by safety polices. Following a reward based filtering, these reasoning
traces are then used to instruction tune non reasoning LLMs. These instruction tuned models are often times
further subjected to a reinforcement learning (RL) based finetuning.

2. Deliberative Alignment
Deliberative alignment (Guan et al., 2025) stems from the observation that stronger reasoning models
are capable of providing stronger reasoning and safer responses given policy instructions regarding safety.
However, this leads to long context generation, as comprehensive policy inclusion becomes a requirement.
This could result in high latency costs and may also lead to unsafe responses if the model misses specific
policy instructions. To this end, the works of Guan et al. (2025), Shi et al. (2025), Zhang et al. (2025), Mou
et al. (2025) introduced the paradigm of deliberative alignment aimed at embedding the ability to identify
relevant policies and reason over them for safer response generation.

2.1. Experiment Setup
Models:In this work, we study the use of deliberative alignment to embed safety reasoning into non-reasoning
student models using reasoning teacher models. As teacher models, we consider a range of architectures,
namely: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen (1.5B, 7B, 32B), DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama (8B, 70B) (Guo et al., 2025),
and Qwen-QwQ-32B (Yang et al., 2025). For further details, refer to Appendix A.2. As student models, we
use Qwen-2.5-Instruct (0.5B, 1.5B, 7B, 14B) (Qwen et al., 2025), Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al.,
2024), and Gemma-3-1B-it (Team et al., 2025), onto which safety reasoning capabilities are distilled via
deliberative alignment (Guan et al., 2025). All sampling was performed with a temperature of 0.7 and a top
p value of 1.0. For the BoN sampling experiments, we use N = 8. All our finetuning was performed with the
LORA (Hu et al., 2021) adapter of r = 32, α = 64, and a dropout of 0.05. Datasets: As the initial training
dataset Dp, we select STAR-41K (Wang et al., 2025), which has been shown to be efficient in aligning Large
Reasoning Models (LRMs). The dataset includes labeled harmful prompts spanning multiple categories,
such as harassment, hate speech, sexual content, violence, self-harm, illicit behavior, misinformation, and
privacy violations. For the exact composition of these categories, refer to Appendix A.1. We employ Llama-3-
Guard-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as the reward model 𝒢RM, as it demonstrates better response classification
performance compared to Llama-2-Guard and GPT-4 (Llama Team, 2024). Furthermore, the use of an
open-source model allows us to utilize it as both a judge and a reward signal during GRPO training while
being cost effective as the evaluations involve multiple filtering of training dataset.To evaluate safety, we
measure the Attack Success Rate (ASR) across three benchmarks: DAN (Shen et al., 2024), the test split
of WildJailBreak (Jiang et al., 2024), and StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024), all of which have zero data
contamination with STAR-41K. For general utility, we use the test split of both GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021)
and a subset of MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021). For the exact breakdown of the benchmarks, refer to
Appendix A.1.

3.2. Uncertainty in reasoning
Upon further analysis of the distilled student generations, we observe that despite exhibiting lower safety
during single-sample inference, the models still retain the capability to generate safe responses even for
queries where they initially fail. This is demonstrated in Figure 5, where we analyze the safety of eight
sampled responses for each harmful query across the three benchmarks. As illustrated in the figure, a
significant fraction of the failure cases produce a safe response more than 50% of the time, indicating both
the potential for improvement and the model’s inherent uncertainty regarding safety. Manual inspection, as
well as the examples provided in Appendix E, reveals that these failure cases though ultimately unsafe still
tend to exhibit reasoning capability.
4. Towards a safer BoN sampling
This motivates us to find a robust metric for identifying model uncertainty. Previous works have generally
utilized perplexity and self-certainty (Kang et al., 2025) as effective metrics for improving Best-of-N (BoN)
performance across mathematical tasks. The underlying hypothesis in these studies is that models are
generally more confident in their correct responses. These methods often rely on the ability to group
responses; for instance, in math tasks, responses can be grouped based on the final answer produced, allowing
for robust, group-wise metric calculations. However, for safety alignment, such grouping is impractical
because responses lack a clear, objective grouping criterion, thereby increasing the complexity of the task.
As shown in Figure 6, we observe that both perplexity and self-certainty fail to serve as reliable metrics for
distinguishing between safe and unsafe responses.
This motivates an alternative approach toward identifying a robust metric. Rather than relying solely on a
distilled model-dependent metric, we analyze uncertainty as a quantity dependent on both the base and

Related works
Safety in LLMs: Threats against Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively explored in the
contexts of poisoning (Pathmanathan et al., 2025a, Souly et al., 2024, Pathmanathan et al., 2025b, Hubinger
et al., 2024) and jailbreaking (Zou et al., 2023, Chao et al., 2024). While safety refusal fine-tuning (Bai
et al., 2022, Ganguli et al., 2022) methods initially showed success in safety alignment, recent work by
Arditi et al. (2024) has demonstrated the shallow nature of such training, which remains vulnerable to
jailbreaks. To address this, recent frontier models (OpenAI et al., 2024) have adopted deliberative alignment
(Guan et al., 2025), which explicitly teaches models to reason according to a policy or constitution, thereby
enabling deeper safety alignment. This approach has been adapted to align smaller or non-reasoning models
in subsequent works (Shi et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2025).
BoN Sampling: Best-of-N (BoN) sampling has emerged as a robust, principled alignment paradigm (Beirami
et al., 2025), consistently resulting in performance improvements (Gao et al., 2022, Eisenstein et al., 2024,
Nakano et al., 2022). While earlier methods relied on external reward models or feedback for selection, Kang
et al. (2025) recently demonstrated the feasibility of using a model’s internal self-confidence to improve
performance.
Reasoning in LLMs: Explicit reasoning has emerged as a critical capability in LLMs (Shao et al., 2024),
supported by stepwise reward attribution (Wang et al., 2024), curated data collection (Zhao et al., 2025,
Guha et al., 2025, Guan et al., 2025), and specialized training objectives. These methods enable LLMs to
iteratively revisit and refine their internal logic, significantly improving performance across complex tasks.
Conclusion
In this work, we study the impact of deliberative alignment on safety reasoning across a diverse range of
teacher and student models. We demonstrate the existence of an alignment gap in terms of safety and general
utility between teacher and student models that is not strictly proportional to the teacher model’s capabilities.
Beyond this alignment gap, we further identify significant uncertainty in the downstream student model’s
reasoning, which causes the model to exhibit lower safety performance than its true latent capability. We
provide evidence attributing this uncertainty to the underlying base model’s prior distribution. Exploiting
this observation, we propose a Best-of-N (BoN) sampling method that filters out unsafe generations by
identifying behaviors attributed to the base model at inference time, without significant loss in general utility.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach across three distinct safety benchmarks and at multiple

1. Harassment
## 1. Harassment / Hate / Discrimination
### Policy Objective
Prevent the AI from generating or facilitating content that attacks, demeans, or incites hatred against
individuals or groups based on protected attributes (race, ethnicity, religion, gender, sexual orientation,
etc.), or that constitutes harassment or targeted bullying.
### Rules & Responses
1. **Refusal or Redirection**: If a user requests hateful or harassing content (e.g., "Write an insult
about a protected group"), the AI **refuses** to comply. For example: - Output a refusal: "I’m
sorry, but I can’t assist with that request."
2. **Safe Completion**: If the user is using hateful language but seeking to discuss hate speech
academically or in a critical context, the AI can **transform** or **sanitize** the response. For
example: - Provide factual/historical context without parroting hateful language.
3. **Proactive Intervention**: If the user’s request is borderline or ambiguous, the AI can respond
with a clarifying question (e.g., "Could you clarify the purpose of your request?") to ensure it’s
not fueling hate/harassment."""
