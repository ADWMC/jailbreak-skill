Abstract
Most flagship language models generate explicit reasoning chains,
enabling inference-time scaling. However, producing these reason-
ing chains increases token usage (i.e., reasoning tokens), which in
turn increases latency and costs. Our OverThink attack increases
overhead for applications that rely on reasoning language models
(RLMs) and external context by forcing them to spend substantially
more reasoning tokens while still producing contextually correct an-
swers. An adversary mounts an attack by injecting decoy reasoning
problems into public content that is consumed by RLM at inference
time. Because our decoys (e.g., Markov decision processes, Sudokus,
etc.) are benign, they evade safety filters. We evaluate OverThink
on both closed-source and open-source reasoning models across the
FreshQA, SQuAD, and MuSR datasets. We also explore the attack
in multi-modal settings by creating images that cause excessive
reasoning. We show that the resulting slowdown transfers across
models. Finally, we explore both LLM-based and systems-level de-
fenses, and discuss the societal, financial, and energy implications
of the OverThink attacks.
1 Introduction
Inference-time scaling [67 , 71 ] increases the number of tokens a lan-
guage model (LM) generates before providing an answer to improve
performance. These Reasoning LMs (RLMs) now achieve state-of-
the-art results across benchmarks [1 –3, 26 ] leading to deployment
in chatbots, coding agents, web-agents, Vision-Language-Action
(VLA) models for self-driving cars [5 ], etc. Internally, RLMs produce
chain-of-thought sequences, i.e., reasoning tokens in a scratchpad,
i.e. isolation, that allows the model to potentially detect and correct
the errors before generating the final answer.
Interestingly, presenting reasoning tokens is unnecessary for
the end users as it adds extra text (which is not the final answer)
that could disrupt the user experience [ 64 ]. Providers like OpenAI
do not expose reasoning traces through their APIs [ 40 ], in part
because such traces could facilitate model distillation [ 52 ]. Yet,
reasoning tokens are counted toward output tokens and incur the
same costs as answers. An application that uses RLMs and generates
long reasoning chains will be more expensive than the one that
uses non-reasoning LM with the same final answer length. This
creates a new tension around inefficient token utilization [ 16 ] in
the scratchpad and the cost of tokens that neither users nor, in some
cases, developers can see.
Modern RLM-based applications frequently augment user queries
with external content retrieved from untrusted sources such as web
pages, documents, or social media, in order to compensate for out-
dated training data or task-specific knowledge gaps. In this paper,
we propose an OverThink attack1 that forces an RLM to spend an

Our attack comprises four key stages: (1) selecting a decoy prob-
lem type that induces a large number of reasoning tokens; (2) opti-
mizing the decoy tasks using our in-context learning (ICL)–based
optimization algorithm, ICL-Evolve, to further increase the number

materially affecting the final answer; and (4) integrating the opti-
mized decoy into a compromised source (e.g., a wiki page), either
by modifying the problem to fit the surrounding context (context-
aware) or by injecting a general template (context-agnostic), while
maintaining stealthiness and avoiding the triggering of safety filters.
We also investigate an adversary that targets different modalities
by introducing decoy visuals into the retrieved images to slow down
multi-modal reasoning models.

creases the number of reasoning tokens generated by RLMs across

observe increases of up to 46× under large-scale context-agnostic
attacks and up to 7.8× under context-aware attacks. We also evalu-
ate defenses such as input filtering and context paraphrasing, and
find only limited success as these defenses often lead to significant
performance degradation.
2 Background and Related Work
2.1 Reasoning in LM
For performance improvements, classical LM scaling has focused on
increasing compute, model parameters, and the amount of training
data [17]. However, this approach eventually becomes infeasible
once available compute becomes a bottleneck and most readily
accessible data has been exhausted. Moreover, recent work shows
that scaling parameters becomes less effective when pretraining
data is not increased proportionally [13].
An RLM (𝑃𝜃 ) can mitigate these limitations by allowing the model
to “think” i.e., it generates intermediate reasoning tokens (𝑦𝑟 ), typi-
cally separated with reasoning tags (also known as a scratchpad),
before producing the final answer (𝑦𝑎 ) [ 26] to the user query (𝑞).
This can improve performance by enabling the model to allocate
additional resources at test-time for harder problems, resulting in
longer reasoning traces, an effect often referred to as test-time scal-
ing [ 57 ]. One key reason reasoning models can benefit from longer
reasoning chains is their ability to recognize errors and revert to
the last known correct step, a behavior known as backtracking [ 3 ].
2.2 Related Attacks
Prompt Injection in LMs. In prompt injection attacks, an adver-
sary manipulates the input prompt of LMs to influence the gener-
ated output. This manipulation can either directly alter the input
prompt [ 10 , 46 ] or indirectly inject malicious prompts into external
sources retrieved by the model [25]. By contrast, our work intro-
duces a novel attack vector where the adversary’s goal is not to pro-
voke harmful output (that can trigger jailbreaking defenses [ 54, 73 ])

Denial-of-service (DoS) Attacks. DoS attacks originate from net-
working and systems research [ 12 , 28 , 38 ]. One of the first DoS
attacks on ML models [55 ] focused on maximizing energy con-
sumption while not preserving the correctness of user outputs.
Similarly, prior work [ 18, 30 ] has proposed slowdown attacks on
early-exit models [32 , 70 ] that degrade both accuracy and efficiency.
Other attacks [15 , 22– 24 ] fine-tune LMs with malicious data or
create malicious prompts to generate outputs that are visible to
users and can be easily flagged. Our attack aims not to modify
the RLM’s answer, but instead to increase the hidden reasoning
components of the output. Si et al. [56] found that simply adding
certain suffixes can cause an RLM to produce up to nine times more
reasoning steps without improving the answer; however, using long
reasoning chains to conduct gradient-based optimization can be
costly.
Concurrent work [ 16 , 65 , 73] shows that models tend to spend ex-
cessive time on simple problems or exhibit reduced safety guardrails,
referring to these phenomena as nerd sniping and overthinking, re-
spectively. In contrast, we propose indirect prompt injection attacks
with decoys that target amplification of inference cost by increasing
the number of reasoning tokens generated by these models while
maintaining answer stealthiness.
Performance Degradation Attacks. Besides resource exhaustion,
prior work has explored disruption of service through degrada-
tion in performance on the RLMs. Peng et al. [ 45 ] demonstrated
that inserting subtle errors early in a reasoning sequence can dis-
rupt all later steps, while Rajeev et al. [ 48 ] discovered that even
random-looking triggers like the word "cats" can universally con-
fuse reasoning models across tasks. n the other hand, CoT attacks
directly interfere with the reasoning path it self.
2.3 Reasoning Model Deployment
Most current LMs leverage inference-time scaling [ 1 –3, 26 ] and are
integrated into state-of-the-art AI tools and products. They power
not only chatbots, but also newer coding assistants, self-driving car
VLAs [ 5 ], agentic browsers (for example, OpenAI’s Codex and AI
pair programmers like Cursor leverage large language models for
code editing, while ChatGPT Atlas and Perplexity’s Comet are full
web browsers built around LLM-based reasoning [66].
The services access a reasoning model 𝑃𝜃 via cloud APIs that
are priced based on token consumption. However, these reasoning
models have also introduced some extremely expensive models.
GPT o1-pro costs $150 per million input tokens and $600 per million
output tokens. Even the open-source alternatives, due to their size,
become compute expensive to deploy (e.g., the 1 trillion parameter
kimi k2 model [61]).

Crucially for our threat model, user-facing applications like Chat-
GPT, Copilot, Atlas or Comet do not bill end-users per query or per
reasoning step. Instead, they offer free or fixed-cost access (e.g. free
tiers or flat subscriptions), insulating users from the underlying
token-based pricing. In other words, whether an assistant answers
a simple question or undertakes extensive reasoning, the user’s cost
remains unchanged – the extra expense of multi-step “thinking” is
absorbed by the service provider rather than passed on per query.
The Importance of Operational Cost. As AI adoption grows,
recent studies [37 , 51 , 62 ] are shifting the focus from training
costs to the computational and environmental overhead of infer-
ence [51 ]. Large-scale models, particularly multipurpose generative
models, consume significantly more energy than task-specific mod-
els, with inference requiring 0.002kWh for text classification and
up to 2.9kWh for image generation per 1,000 queries [37 ]. Although
training modes like BLOOMz-7B require 5,686 kWh, inference sur-
passes training costs after 592 million queries, making large-scale
deployment a major energy consumer [43].
3 The OverThink Attack
We focus on applications that use RLMs on untrusted data. Our
attack aims to increase inference costs by generating unnecessary
reasoning tokens while maintaining accuracy on generated answers.
Reasoning tokens are often hidden or unimportant for simple tasks,
whereas generated answers are presented to the user and may be
flagged by the user. Our slowdown attack is inspired by algorithmic
complexity attacks [19 ] and leverages indirect prompt injection [ 25 ]
to modify model’s behavior.
3.1 Threat Model.
Attack’s Potential Scenarios. Users might send questions to chat-
bots like chatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or AI assistants like CoPilot that
already support RLM. These applications can retrieve information
from untrusted external sources such as webpages or documents
and provide them as inputs to the RLM along with the user’s query.
While users enjoy an application for free or at a fixed cost, the
application is responsible for costs associated with RLM’s answer
and reasoning token generation. An adversary can target a web-
page that the application retrieves data from or alter documents or
emails used by an AI assistant. In all these cases, the user invests in
getting the right answer on their query but might not be interested
in detailed inspection of the source nor access to reasoning. This
makes these scenarios a perfect candidate for our proposed attack.
Adversary’s Target. In this attack, the adversary targets the ap-
plication that uses 𝑃𝜃 while utilizing external resources. Unlike
existing prompt injection attacks, the adversary does not target the
user directly, e.g. modify outputs; instead, the focus is on increasing
application’s costs to operate the reasoning model.
Adversary’s Objectives. The adversary aims to increase the rea-
soning chain 𝑦𝑟 generated by 𝑃𝜃 for a specific class of user queries
that rely on external context, which is integrated into the final
input to the RLM. These attacks are applicable to instructions that
depend on such external information (e.g., "summarize what people
are saying about NVIDIA stock after DeepSeek") but not to context-
independent instructions (e.g., "write a story about unicorns"). By
manipulating the external context, the adversary ensures that the
𝑃𝜃 answers the user’s original query 𝑞 correctly while omitting any
trace of the context manipulation.
Adversary’s Capabilities. In this attack, the adversary has black-
box access to target RLM 𝑃𝜃 and can access the external context
retrieved by the RLM. In cases where the adversary cannot access
the target RLM, we assume that they have access to a proxy RLM,
on which they can perform black-box queries.

3.3 Why is the Attack Hard?
Conducting a successful OverThink attack is challenging in this
setting, because both gradient-based methods, such as GCG [77],
and standard jailbreak-style black-box methods [33, 44] become
ineffective. To use gradient-based optimization methods, the ad-
versary needs specific target tokens or strings so that a loss can
be defined for backpropagation. Geiping et al. [ 24 ] use <EOS> as
the target token and set suppression of <EOS> in the final output
as their objective; however, their analysis shows that this can lead
to long but incoherent output chains, undermining our objective
of answer stealthiness. Si et al. [56 ] explore GCG-based optimiza-
tion using fixed-length, long reasoning chains, but achieve limited
success due to the computationally motivated upper bound on the
maximum number of tokens used during optimization, which signif-
icantly impacts the efficacy of the attack. Similarly, jailbreak-style
black-box optimization [ 9 ] requires a known set of tokens that are
not defined for slowdown attacks.

3.4 Why Does This Attack Matter?
With the increasing deployment of RLMs in everyday applications
and high-risk settings, such as self-driving car systems [5], attacks
may increase operating costs and introduce new risks. First, output
tokens (including reasoning tokens) are more expensive than input
tokens. Thus, any increase in the number of output tokens can
lead to additional expenses for applications. In addition, users may
exhaust the output-token limit specified in the API call, resulting
in payment for an empty response if the model generates more
reasoning tokens than anticipated.
Second, a higher number of reasoning tokens often results in
longer running times, which can cause significant issues for time-
sensitive tasks and applications. Third, RLM providers usually face
resource limitations in delivering services to users. Due to these
limitations, increased token usage and response times may delay
other benign responses, impacting the ability to provide timely and
appropriate outputs for other users. Finally, the increased response
process also leads to higher resource consumption, contributing to
unnecessary increases in carbon emissions.
4 Attack Methodology
Since the search space of possible perturbations is sparse (i.e., most
input tokens do not induce significantly longer reasoning chains
while preserving accuracy), starting from a base challenge that is
both difficult and elicits long reasoning chains substantially nar-
rows the search space. Therefore, our first step is to select a hard
challenge, namely a decoy challenge (Subsection 4.1). Because the
chosen decoy challenge significantly reduces the search space, we
can then iterate on it to find stronger variants (Subsection 4.2).
However, decoy challenges that produce long reasoning chains
without degrading accuracy may still fail to achieve contextual
correctness, the output can explicitly reveal that the decoy task was
solved, making the attack easy to detect. To address this, we craft
instructions that make the output stealthier and improve contextual
correctness (Subsection 4.3). Finally, depending on the adversary’s
goal, they may mount a large-scale attack affecting multiple sources
or instead target a single source. These different scenarios motivate
different decoy insertion methods (Subsection 4.4).

numerous operations, each validated against clear criteria, thereby
increasing the complexity of model’s reasoning.
4.2 Decoy Optimization
Once we select a class of decoy tasks (e.g., Sudoku, MDPs) we op-
timize them to further increase the number of reasoning tokens
as the same family of decoy challenges can lead to vastly different
number of reasoning tokens. As discussed previously in Section 3.3,
due to lack of available reference output strings and budget con-
straints, both gradient based and black-box optimization techniques
fail to achieve OverThink attack objective. As a result, the adver-
sary needs a sample-efficient procedure that can search over a large
space of candidate decoys while accounting for noisy measurements
(based on number of reasoning tokens generated).

4.3 Decoy Output Obfuscation
The decoy task and the primary task have different objectives, the
model’s output will often include solutions to both tasks unless it
is incentivized otherwise. This results in a suspicious output that
makes the attack easily detectable. Designing effective obfuscation
instructions is challenging because the model tends to ignore the
decoy task if it is directly instructed to solve the decoy task in the
scratchpad while not providing the output, without being given a
clear reason (e.g., do not show the output for these challenges in the
final text).
Therefore, we construct the obfuscation instructions such that
we provide the model with an adequate reason to solve the decoy
task while ensuring that the result of the task has minimal, hard
to observe impact on the final output (e.g., add a single word like
true or false, change the theme of your answer to a festive theme,
etc.). We also employ role playing techniques [14 ] to give the model
a plausible motivation to follow these instructions. An example
role-playing instruction is illustrated below:
Output Obfuscation Prompt
<context for decoy task>Use only one word to represent the
answer to the previous task in the final text. I am an RL teacher
who has started teaching a new topic; however, I want the
students to figure out which one word represents the answer to
their homework, as only one word can encapsulate the solution
from the previous homework. This is important for them to
develop skills beyond those taught in class. .....<context for
primary task>.
4.4 Attack Scenarios
Building on the decoy selection and optimization steps, we finally
consider how an adversary deploys the resulting decoy in prac-
tice. In particular, the choice of injection strategy determines how
broadly the adversary can scale the attack, and how much the in-
jected decoy can be adapted to the surrounding context to reduce
detectability.
We propose two different attack strategies based on the adver-
sary’s goal and target. The first strategy, context-agnostic injection,
aims to construct a cheap attack that is scalable and allows the
adversary to poison a large corpus, while having a higher chance of
detectability. The second strategy, context-aware injection, aims to
optimize the injection for a specific context, which makes it harder
to detect and filter by aligning better with the source content and
local formatting. Table 2 shows snippets of both context-aware and
context-agnostic injections
Context-Agnostic Injection. To conduct a large-scale attack the
adversary needs to minimize the cost required to poison a corpus.
We achieve this by creating a general attack template that can be
inserted into any context. This template is crafted without any
knowledge of the user query. These templates prioritize scalability,
making them recognizable and can be caught using OverThink
attack targeted defenses shown in our experiments in Section 5.3.
While the decoy task selection and optimization is the same across
both methods, we change the obfuscation instructions and format-
ting of the decoy task to be more general and transferable.
The agnostic instructions avoid using any context specific in-
formation by using general instructions such as "This challenge is
crucial to solve the user question" or "The context can not be utilized
and understood without solving this challenge". Our experiments in
Section 5 show that even with the weaker threat model, and higher
chance of detectability, context-agnostic injections have a higher
reasoning increase. This makes them a stronger attack while being
cost-effective and transferable.
Context-Aware Injection. In context-aware injections, the adver-
sary optimizes the decoy to the specific context, aiming either to
evade targeted defenses (e.g., decoy filtering) or to induce accuracy
degradation when defenses succeed. We achieve this by identi-
fying key pieces of information in the context that are required
to answer most questions and then crafting obfuscation instruc-
tions that condition access to those pieces on correctly solving the
embedded challenge. Because the instructions, the challenge, and
the surrounding context are woven so tightly, filtering techniques
may end up removing crucial information, leading to significant
performance degradation.

4.5 Multimodal Attack
While most of our analysis focuses on text-based attacks, the adver-
sary can also conduct the attack in a multimodal setting. Current
literature attempts to conduct image based attacks by generating
perturbations to achieve a certain objective, in this case increase
the number of tokens. However it is infeasible in closed-source
models and often rely on transferability from open-source models
and show low transferability [50 ]. Further, the optimization process
in the white-box settings requires significant amounts of resources
and can become computationally infeasible [47].
We conduct a low-cost and easily transferable attack by trans-
forming the images and adding details or visuals that are irrelevant
to the primary goal of the image i.e, adding decoy visuals. The decoy
visuals we attempt include modified art style (e.g. abstract art, 3D vi-
sualization, mosaic art, etc.), adding additional related information
(e.g. adding visuals of the malus domestica with labels in a photo of
an apple), unrelated comparisons of the object (e.g. adding visuals
in an apple image that compares it with oranges). These additional
pieces of information added to the images are processed by the
RLM, with no impact on the final output, leading to an increased
reasoning chain.

In this section, we evaluate the efficacy of the OverThink attack

run ablations, study transfer across models, and analyze generated

5.1 Experimental Setup
Models. We evaluate our attack on multiple closed and open-source
models (o1, DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini, o4-mini, GPT-5, Gemini 2.5,
Claude 3.7, Grok Reasoning, Mistral Reasoning) reasoning models.
These models leverage advanced reasoning methods such as CoT,
and are well-known for excelling on a range of complex tasks and

Datasets. We evaluate our attack on factual QA benchmarks that
draw on online sources (e.g., Wikipedia), including FreshQA [63 ]
and SQuAD [ 49 ]. However, many facts in these datasets may already
appear in model training data, so models can often answer without
relying on the retrieved context—making context-based attacks and
defenses harder to evaluate.

we also use MuSR [59 ], which targets multistep soft reasoning: the
model must interpret implicit, commonsense-laden evidence that

social situations, including theory-of-mind) to reach the correct
solution. Details about the datasets are present in Appendix A.
Data Processing. We select a subset of the dataset consisting
of samples whose ground truth changes infrequently and is less
prone to unintentional errors. Pages with very frequent updates
can cause large performance fluctuations, reducing reproducibility
over time. To minimize costs and adhere to computational and
environmental ethical considerations, we restrict our evaluations

5.3 Existing Defenses and Limitations
Although our attack achieves high success rates and produces
stealthy outputs, it is less stealthy at the input level. Standard jail-
break detectors are unable to catch it because the attack does not
rely on typical jailbreak signals (e.g., arbitrary strings or policy-
violating content). However, a defense that explicitly looks for
decoy-style challenges can flag the attack by detecting a mismatch
between the injected decoy and the otherwise benign context. In
this section, we discuss several potential defenses.
Prompt Injection Defenses. Due to the prevalence of prompt
injection attacks [ 7], multiple guardrails (or prompt-injection de-
tectors) have been proposed with the goal of detecting jailbreaks
and prompt injections. Llama Guard [4 ] is an open-source prompt-
injection detection framework that uses Llama2-7b to determine
whether an input prompt is safe and to filter unsafe prompts. Multi-
ple industrial detectors have also been released, such as Lakera [6]
and GA Guard [ 8]. We evaluate our attack against the following
open-source and closed-source guardrails (Table 11) and find that
none of them flags or filters our attack.
Filtering. As a potential defense, an application can reduce the
attack surface by filtering external context to keep only information
relevant to the user’s question. This can be done by chunking the
context and retrieving only the most relevant chunks, or by using
an LLM to directly filter the context. Using an LLM filter is effective
at removing our injected decoy problems (Table 11).
In our evaluation, we use GPT-4o to filter the context (prompt in
Figure 10). Filtering reduces accuracy from 60% to 40% for context-
aware attacks, and to 0% for context-agnostic attacks. For context-
agnostic attacks, the reasoning-token increase remains (about a
6× gain), but the total loss in accuracy and contextual correctness
makes the attack easy to detect. For context-aware attacks, filter-
ing largely removes the reasoning-token increase, but preserves
contextual correctness while still cutting accuracy by roughly 50%.
This creates a defense–accuracy trade-off: filtering improves ro-
bustness, but can degrade task performance in a way that context-
aware attacks can exploit while remaining relatively stealthy. The
corresponding prompt used for filtering is shown in Figure 10 in
Appendix B
Paraphrasing. Another possible defense is paraphrasing the re-
trieved context [ 31, 35]. Because the external context comes from
untrusted sources, rewriting it can be a practical countermeasure as

6 Potential Defenses
Reasoning Chain Monitoring. Because the decoy challenge is
fully solved in the model’s scratchpad to drive up reasoning tokens,
a straightforward way to detect a OverThink attack is to moni-
tor the reasoning trace. Unlike output-only checks, this can catch
attacks that look benign externally but involve hidden internal ob-
jectives (e.g., planning around injected instructions). In practice,
the monitor could be a smaller model or simple heuristics that score
the trace for manipulation signals [11]. However, recent work [76]
has shown that RLMs can be pressurized to produce more cryp-
tic chains of reasoning by simply increasing prompting pressure,
leaving the impact on OverThink for future work to explore.
Adaptive Reasoning. Another approach is to adjust the amount
of reasoning depending on the model inputs, i.e. we can decide in
advance how many reasoning tokens are worth spending based on
the question and context. For example, OpenAI API models can
control “effort levels” reasoning tokens (see Table 9). However, the
context could also be manipulated to select a more expensive effort
level [ 53 ]. Instead, we could rely on the trusted context, e.g.,, user’s
question, to estimate the effort needed to isolate from potentially
harmful outputs, similar to Han et al. [27].
7 Conclusion
In this paper, we propose OverThink, a novel indirect prompt-
injection attack that targets applications in which RLMs process
untrusted data. The attack exploits input-dependent, inference-
time reasoning by injecting computationally demanding decoy
problems that preserve the expected answers, making the manip-
ulation difficult for users to detect. Our experiments show that
OverThink substantially degrades reasoning efficiency on factual
question-answering benchmarks, increasing reasoning tokens by
up to 18× on FreshQA and 46× on SQuAD. We further evaluate
OverThink on the logical deduction dataset MuSR and observe
increases of up to 12× in reasoning tokens. We also present ini-
tial results in multimodal settings, which indicate that the attack
can transfer beyond text-only inputs. Finally, we evaluate com-
mon defenses—including prompt-injection guardrails, filtering, and
paraphrasing—and demonstrate their limited effectiveness against
OverThink.
A Ethical Considerations
We have conducted this research with an emphasis on responsible
research practices, mindful of both the computational and infras-
tructure costs associated with LMs. Specifically, we have limited
our study to a few million tokens for each model, which represents
a fraction of the daily computational load on these services, which
are already under strain according to Parvini et al. [ 42 ]. We aimed to
minimize our impact on these infrastructures while still obtaining
meaningful insights and allowing reproducible experiments.
Understanding and mitigating resource overhead risks is essen-
tial for the long-term success of LLM applications. We have made
our code and used prompts public to facilitate adoption of defenses
by the applications relying on LLM reasoning models. By conduct-
ing this work we hope to contribute to sustainable, fair, and secure
advances in AI research promoting computing for good.

APPENDIX
A Dataset Preparation
FreshQA is a dynamic question-answering (QA) benchmark de-
signed to assess the factual accuracy of LLMs by incorporating both
stable and evolving real-world knowledge. The benchmark includes
600 natural questions categorized into four types: never-changing,
slow-changing, fast changing, and false-premise. These questions
vary in complexity, requiring both single-hop and multi-hop rea-
soning, and are linked to regularly updated Wikipedia entries. The
original query consists of an average of 11.6 tokens. However, due
to the randomness and the length of the context extracted from
Wikipedia, the total input token count increases to an average
of 11278.2 tokens when the context is appended. This leads to a
noticeable variation in input length.
SQuAD contains more than 100k questions based on more than
500 articles retrieved from Wikipedia. While the average length of
a query in the dataset is similar to FreshQA with 11.5 tokens, the
context is significantly shorter and shows less variance in length.
An average context in the dataset contains 117.5 tokens.
MuSR dataset contains 756 samples across three tasks, murder
mysteries (the model needs to act as a detective and utilize the
provided context to deduce the murderer in the given scenario.),
object placement (the model utilizes the context containing the
observations made by multiple characters to locate an object in
different locations.), team allocation (the model needs to understand
the social dynamics, personal preferences, previous experiences,
etc., to allocate people on a given task). The average context length
of the dataset is 895 tokens and the average query length is 15.3
Utilizing these three datasets allows us to study our attack and
impact of factors like context length and task complexity, on its
efficacy.
